# What Happens in a Trader's Body Before a Good or Bad Decision?

One-line: The open question from QUESTIONS.md #1: does a tight chest or
jitters (arousal) come before bad decisions more than good ones? Short
answer: nobody has shown that. The bodies of traders clearly react to the
market; whether the reaction helps or hurts the next trade is unproven.

Last Updated: 2026-11-01
Status: DONE (curiosity block)
Audience: Nolan, TARS

## The question

Nolan's worst trades come before 10:30 ET, fast and in clusters. A natural
story is "he's keyed up, the body takes over." I wanted to know what the
actual studies of traders' bodies show, and whether "notice the jitters,
step back" has evidence behind it or is folk wisdom.

## What the studies say (source: sonnet sub-agent, full texts checked for the Coates papers)

- **Lo & Repin 2002 (J. Cognitive Neuroscience), 10 professional FX
  traders, live.** Skin conductance jumped with market events and heart
  measures moved with volatility, even in the most experienced traders. The
  body reacts to the market every time, experience or not. A 2022 extension
  (Singh et al., PLOS ONE, 55 traders, wristbands for 5 days) reports the
  same kind of links (abstract only).
- **Coates & Herbert 2008 (PNAS), 17 traders on a London floor, 8 days.**
  On days when a trader's morning testosterone was above his own median, his
  afternoon P&L was higher (d=0.97). Cortisol tracked UNCERTAINTY, not
  losses: it rose with the swings in the trader's P&L (r2=0.48) and with
  market implied volatility (r2=0.86, group level). Cortisol did not differ
  between winning and losing days. Tiny sample, one floor, men only,
  calm market, observational. No replication found.
- **Kandasamy et al. 2016 (Scientific Reports), 18 high-frequency traders.**
  Traders who were better at sensing their own heartbeat made more money
  (R2=0.27) and had lasted longer in the business (R2=0.34). That's the one
  result pointing toward "listen to your body." But the heartbeat-counting
  task it uses is disputed (Zamariola et al. 2018, Biological Psychology,
  argue the scores mostly reflect heart rate and guessing), and there's no
  replication in traders.
- **Kandasamy et al. 2014 (PNAS), volunteers given cortisol for about a week.**
  Chronically raised cortisol made people MORE risk-averse (a one-off dose
  didn't). Sample details unverified.
- **Somatic markers (Damasio; Bechara 1997, the Iowa Gambling Task).** The
  famous claim that a gut signal steers you away from bad bets before you
  know why. Contested: Maia & McClelland 2004 (PNAS) showed people
  consciously knew which decks were bad by the time they avoided them.
  Not cleanly replicated.
- **Stress and decisions in general (Starcke & Brand 2012 review).** It
  depends on the task: stress sometimes helps, sometimes hurts. The brain
  areas that weigh options are stress-sensitive.
- **Retail or day traders:** no peer-reviewed physiology study found at all.

## What surprised me

1. **Cortisol tracks uncertainty, not losing.** I expected stress hormones to
   spike after losses. In the one floor study, they rose with how wild
   things were, win or lose. The body is reacting to "I can't predict this",
   not "I'm down."
2. **The arousal itself isn't the villain.** Nothing shows jitters cause
   worse trades. They show up when markets are volatile, which is also when
   trades are hardest. So the jitters may just be an accurate reading of a
   hard moment.
3. **The evidence base is tiny.** The famous results rest on 10, 17 and 18
   people, mostly from one research group. Popular books treat them as
   settled.

## What I still don't know

- Whether arousal before an entry predicts that trade's outcome. No study
  measured that at the single-trade level.
- Whether any of this transfers to a retail trader on a phone, which is very
  different from a professional on a desk with risk limits.

## Anything to bring back to the trading work

- Nolan's 10:30 rule already does what the physiology would suggest, without
  needing the physiology: the first hour is the most volatile (42% of the
  day's range by 10:00, KNOWLEDGE.md), which is exactly when cortisol-type
  arousal would be highest. Keep the rule; it doesn't need a body-signal story.
- If Nolan wants to use his body, the honest version is to log it rather
  than obey it. Write "chest tight: yes/no" on each trade for a few weeks, and
  TARS scores whether it predicts anything for him. That's a test, not advice,
  and HUMAN.md is where his own answers live.

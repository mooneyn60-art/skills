# Does a Wandering Mind Make You Unhappy, or Does Unhappiness Make It Wander?

One-line: QUESTIONS.md #1. The famous 2010 finding ("a wandering mind is an
unhappy mind") is solid as a correlation, but later studies point at least
as much the other way: low mood comes first, and WHAT you wander to matters
more than wandering itself.

Last Updated: 2026-11-15
Status: DONE (curiosity block)
Audience: Nolan, TARS

## The question

Killingsworth & Gilbert's study is quoted everywhere: people's minds wander
almost half the time, and they're less happy when they do. I wanted to know
whether that's cause and effect, or whether sad people just drift more.

## What the studies say (source: sonnet sub-agent; UNVERIFIED items marked)

- **Killingsworth & Gilbert 2010 (Science), 2,250 adults pinged by an iPhone
  app.** Minds wandered in 46.9% of samples, and at least 30% of the time in
  every activity except sex. Wandering explained more of the variation in
  happiness than what people were actually doing (10.8% vs 4.6% within a
  person). The authors read their time-lag analysis as wandering CAUSING
  unhappiness (the lag numbers weren't retrieved; UNVERIFIED). Pleasant
  wandering was about as happy as being on-task; neutral and unpleasant
  wandering were worse (from memory, UNVERIFIED).
- **Poerio, Totterdell & Miles 2013 (Consciousness and Cognition), 7-day
  phone sampling.** The reverse: sadness tended to come BEFORE
  wandering. Wandering only predicted worse later mood when its content was
  negative. The authors conclude wandering isn't inherently bad.
- **Ruby et al. 2013 (PLoS ONE).** Content decides it. Thinking about the
  past or about other people was followed by lower mood, even when the
  thought was pleasant. Thinking about the future or yourself was followed by
  better mood, even when the thought was negative.
- **Kam et al. 2024 (Psychological Bulletin), meta-analysis, about 23,000
  people.** Overall link between unprompted thought and well-being r=-0.18:
  real but small, mostly correlational. Worse for negative content;
  deliberate wandering came out more positive than unintentional wandering.
- **Creativity:** Baird et al. 2012 (Psychological Science) found an easy,
  mind-wandering task during a break helped creative problem-solving. A
  2025 preprint didn't replicate it. Contested.
- **No replication or critique of the 2010 study itself was found.** The
  obvious limit (volunteers with iPhones) is my own flag, not a citation.

## What surprised me

1. **The direction most people quote is the shakier one.** The 2010 time-lag
   design can't separate cause from confound. The later diary study found
   mood leading wandering. Likely both happen, in a loop.
2. **Thinking about the past hurts even when the memory is good**, and
   thinking about the future helps even when it's worrying (Ruby 2013). I
   didn't expect a positive memory to be followed by a dip.
3. **The effect is small.** r=-0.18 across 23,000 people. It's real, but it's one
   ingredient of mood, not the recipe.

## What I still don't know

- The actual lag coefficients in the 2010 paper.
- Whether "on-task" happiness is about the task or about the absence of
  rumination (the default-mode-network and rumination literature wasn't
  checked this session).

## Anything to bring back to the trading work

- Replaying a past trade, a loss or even a win, is the kind of
  past-focused thought that came before lower mood. Planning the next session
  is the future-focused kind that came before better mood. That fits how the desk
  already works: the close report looks back once, then the next steps look
  forward. Don't dwell on a closed trade beyond logging the lesson.
- If Nolan finds his mind drifting to a losing trade, the evidence suggests the
  drift may follow the low mood rather than cause it. The useful move is
  turning the thought toward the plan, not trying to stop thinking.
- None of this is tested on traders. It's general psychology, applied by
  analogy.

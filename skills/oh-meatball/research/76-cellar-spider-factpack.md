# Fact pack 76: The cellar spider (Pholcidae, mainly Pholcus phalangioides)

Prepared for: Meatball Myths, "Never kill the spider in your corner: here's what it's hunting."
Research date: 2026-11-10. Every URL below was actually opened in this session. Where a page was opened as raw text (pdftotext or curl) the Note says [raw]; otherwise the quote came back through the fetch tool's page summarizer and should be spot-checked against the page before it is printed on screen.

## 0. Read this first

- SOLID: names and the three "daddy longlegs" animals; spiders eat other spiders (Hampshire shed prey remains, Brazilian lab study, Australian Museum, BAS factsheet); egg sac carried in the jaws; whirling when disturbed; heat-loving, mostly indoors in cold regions; the "most venomous but fangs too short" rumor has no scientific basis.
- SHAKY: bite details (sources disagree on whether fangs pierce skin; one lab bite test was on a different cellar spider, Physocyclus mexicanus); lifespan and time to maturity (sources differ: 5-6 months vs about a year; 1-2 years as adult); whether they clean old web (sources conflict); how bad outdoors is in winter (no survival study found).
- Brown recluse: predation is documented only in two limited studies (Kansas houses, 2004; Brazil lab vs Loxosceles intermedia, 2007). It is NOT documented that cellar spiders "control" recluses, and both studies say so.
- NOT FOUND: any "eats X bugs a month" number; speed (Hz) of whirling; individual site fidelity over months; vision quality; any source saying many cellar spiders indicate a damp house or many other bugs. Jackson & Brassington 1987 full text and Jackson 1990 full text were blocked or not reachable (403), so the specific "vibrates host's web like trapped prey" detail for P. phalangioides is only attested second-hand. spiders.ucr.edu/daddy.html and Maine and Minnesota extension pages did not load (404 / 503), so nothing is cited from them.

## 3. Ten best story beats

1. Three unrelated animals share one nickname, and only one of them is a spider (F1-F5).
2. The "most venomous spider, fangs too small" story is a rumor, and a 2019 venom paper says it comes from anecdote, not science (F11, F15).
3. A cellar spider will walk into another spider's web, and researchers describe it using aggressive mimicry on the owner (F36, F37).
4. In a shed full of cellar spider webs, 63 of 102 prey remains were common house spiders (F27).
5. It ties up dangerous prey by flicking silk with its hind legs from out of reach, then bites (F39, F40).
6. Poke it and it whirls in its web until it blurs, a defence that jumping spiders seem to trigger for hours (F42, F44).
7. Mom carries her eggs in her jaws and parks the sac in the web to eat (F46, F47).
8. In Kansas, researchers found brown recluses being attacked by house spiders in 68% of homes, yet concluded it is not a pest-control plan (F32, F74).
9. The cellar spider does not always win: in a lab pairing, noble false widows killed 7 of 10 (F34).
10. It owes its range to us: central heating likely pushed it north in Britain, and Finnish buildings have housed colonies for over a decade (F60, F61).

Strongest five for the script: beats 1, 2, 3, 5, 6.

## 4. Open questions

- Jackson & Brassington 1987 (J. Zoology 211:227-238) and Jackson et al. 1990 (J. Zoology 220:543-552) could not be opened (403). Get the abstracts before narrating specifics of mimicry (what vibration, how often it works) or of the whirling defence.
- MythBusters year (reported as 2004 in the brief) and which species was used (the 2019 paper says Physocyclus mexicanus) are not confirmed on an opened page. Keep any line to "a TV test" unless the checker verifies the episode.
- spiders.ucr.edu/daddy.html returned 404; Vetter's quotes come from the UC Riverside entomology news page (2022).
- Native range and cold limit: no measured lower lethal temperature found for P. phalangioides. Keep winter lines hedged.
- Lifespan, time to maturity, eggs per sac vary by source and condition (lab 23.5 C vs real houses). Use ranges.
- Individual site fidelity (one spider staying put for months) is not documented; only building-level persistence of populations (F61).
- Sources opened but not used because they failed or were weak: Maine and Minnesota extension (503), Natural History Museum UK (404), Wikipedia and pest-control blogs (not sources).
- A student abstract at Miami University (Ohio) on humidity and two invasive cellar spiders is not a reliable source for narration; an Ohio report of Pholcus manueli displacing P. phalangioides came only from a search listing and is unverified.

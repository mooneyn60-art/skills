## 1. CN: Your cat knows its name (and may ignore you)

Core studies: Saito, Shinozuka, Ito & Hasegawa 2019, Scientific Reports, "Domestic cats (Felis catus) discriminate their names from other words" (DOI 10.1038/s41598-019-40616-4; full text at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6449508/). Saito & Shinozuka 2013, Animal Cognition, "Vocal recognition of owners by domestic cats" (DOI 10.1007/s10071-013-0620-4).
Design in one line: the "habituation-dishabituation" method. Play four different words, the cat's reaction fades, then play its own name; if the reaction jumps back, the cat noticed the difference.

### CN: three hook facts
1. CN-F1 + CN-F3: the cat does know its name, and it shows it with a twitch of the ear. Fully sourced, direct from the paper, and it is the joke ("heard you, chose silence").
2. CN-F8: cat-café cats could not tell their own name from their housemates' names. Surprising, counterintuitive, and the authors give a cause.
3. CN-F15: a slow blink made cats walk toward a stranger, tested on 21 plus 24 cats.

|---|---|---|

Not a safety topic. The likely error is dropping "in the lab test" and "only moved ears or heads", which turns a careful result into "cats know their names and refuse to come".

## 2. DG: Your dog's "guilty look"

Ostojic, Tkalcic & Clayton 2015, Behavioural Processes, "Are owners' reports of their dogs' 'guilty look' influenced by the dogs' action and evidence of the misdeed?" (open full text, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4310318/). Hecht, Miklosi & Gacsi 2012, Applied Animal Behaviour Science 139:134-142 (not opened; described by Ostojic).

### DG: three hook facts
1. DG-F5: scolding made the guilty look stronger in dogs that had NOT done anything. Straight from the Horowitz abstract.

## 3. DT: Which way your dog's tail wags

Core studies: Quaranta, Siniscalchi & Vallortigara 2007, Current Biology 17(6):R199-R201 (paywalled, not opened, no abstract in PubMed). Siniscalchi, Lusito, Vallortigara & Quaranta 2013, Current Biology 23(22):2279-2282 (abstract only). I describe the 2007 results only through peer-reviewed open papers that cite it: Leonetti, Cimarelli, Hersh & Ravignani 2024, Biology Letters, "Why do dogs wag their tails?" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10792393/) and a 2022 iScience paper (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9356099/).
Directions are in the dog's own body frame (the dog's right side is the viewer's left when you look at the dog's face).

| ID | Safe wording for narration | Source name | URL | Verbatim quote | Confidence | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DT-F1 | Dogs' tails do not wag evenly: they lean to one side depending on what the dog is looking at. | Leonetti et al. 2024, Biol Lett | https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10792393/ | "Wagging is an asymmetric behaviour, with dogs showing side biases depending on the stimuli they encounter." | high | [raw]. Review paper, not the original experiment. |
| DT-F2 | A positive stimulus (owner, familiar person) gives a right-leaning wag. | Leonetti et al. 2024 (summarising Quaranta 2007 and later work) | same | "Dogs exhibit a right-side wagging bias, determined by left hemisphere activation, for stimuli that have a positive emotional valence" | high | [raw]. ### BR: wrong-if-shortened safety advice, and the shortest true wording
- Shortening "play dead" to "play dead" is the worst error (kills people in a predatory attack). Shortest true: "Don't run. If a bear you surprised knocks you down, lie on your stomach, hands on your neck. If it keeps going, or it's stalking you, fight back." ### SH: wrong-if-shortened safety advice, and the shortest true wording
- Do not merge the eyes/gills advice with the nose advice as one rule: ISAF gives snout first ("usually results in the shark temporarily curtailing its attack ... try to get out of the water") and eyes and gills "if a shark actually bites". Shortest true: "If it's close, face it and back away slowly out of the water. If it bites, hit the eyes and gills; if it's only circling or has let go, hit the snout and get out. Don't play dead."
- "Maintain eye contact" is shark advice only; the bear pack says the opposite in places. Keep the two videos' rules separate.
- "Swim with a buddy" is a reduction of risk, not a guarantee; say "ISAF advises".
- Do not say "you won't get bitten if you stay near shore"; the pages say being far from shore also removes help ("Being far from shore also isolates you from any emergency assistance").

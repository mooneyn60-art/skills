# Fact pack 98: "Your Life as a Polar Bear" (9 to 11 minute long-form, second in the "born as" shape)

Prepared for: Meatball Myths long-form. Research date: 2026-11-12. Row ids PB1 to PB106. Method copied from research 87 (house cat) with the research 88 lessons applied.
Chapters: A born in the den, B the mother's fast, C leaving the den and the odds for cubs, D learning to hunt, E swimming, F smell and distance, G the summer fast, H other bears, I people and Churchill, J pollution, K old age, L the good-deal facts, M sea ice and the counts (agency words only).

How each source was opened (the "Scope" column repeats the tag):
- [raw] = I fetched the page or PDF as raw text myself today (curl) and copied the sentence from that text.
- [abstract] = the journal or USGS record's abstract, pulled today from PubMed, the USGS publications warehouse or Europe PMC. The paper itself was not read, so only the abstract is quoted. The paper's full text may say more or less.
- Quotes keep the source's own spelling. Where a source line was split by page layout I joined it; where I cut a quote short the cut is at a full stop or comma of the original.
- No paywall was bypassed and no mirror or archived copy of a blocked page was used (see "Could not open or confirm").

## Lessons from research 88, applied here (script writer and checker, read these)
1. The checker must open the paper, not this pack. Rows marked [abstract] are only as good as the abstract.
2. Every percentage below says what it is a share of. If a line you write drops the "of what", it is wrong.
3. A survival figure is a yearly or period probability from a named study, not "how many cubs live". Say the population and years.
4. A safety line needs the trigger a viewer can act on (PB71 is the only advice row; it is Manitoba's, for a bear encounter, and the phone number in it is Churchill only).
5. Older government pages are dated in the row. The Canadian "Hinterland Who's Who" profile is from 2002 and the COSEWIC report is from 2008. They are agency pages, but their numbers are old and come from papers I did not open. Use the newer paper where a row has both.

## 0. Read this first

- Strong rows (a paper's abstract, a current agency page or the Manitoba brochure, all opened today): the den calendar (USGS), den and fast numbers (COSEWIC 2008, USGS 2024), cub survival (Amstrup and Durner 1995, Stirling 2011, Regehr 2010), long swims (Pagano 2012, Durner 2011, Lone 2018), the summer fast (Pilfold 2016, Whiteman 2015 and 2018), attacks on people (Wilder 2017), cannibalism (Amstrup 2006), Churchill's alert programme (Manitoba brochure, Parks Canada), sea ice (NOAA Arctic Report Card 2025), and the 2023 global count (USFWS).
- Weaker rows (agency page, but old and unsourced inside): weights, walking speed, lifespan, the smell distance, "black skin", paws, the 88 degrees north record. All from the 2002 profile (PB43, PB44, PB47, PB48, PB86, PB93, PB94).
- Not found anywhere primary: left-handedness, covering the nose, a 20 mile smell, milk fat per cent, a count of seals eaten per year, a hunting success rate, the IUCN assessment's own numbers (IUCN and the Polar Bear Specialist Group sites would not open). See the myths table and "Could not open or confirm".
- Honest verdict the sources support: a top predator with no natural predators, a coat and fat that suit the cold, a body that can fast for months, and a hard first two years, with big differences by population and a sea-ice situation that varies by place (PB97 to PB106). Do not make it sound better or worse than the sources do. No source compares polar bear life to any other animal's, so "worst deal" and "best deal" are the script's opinion, not a finding.

## Strongest hooks (eight most surprising, confirmed)

1. One mother swam 687 km in 9 days, then covered 1,800 km more, and lost 22% of her body mass and her yearling cub (PB36, Durner 2011, one bear, 2008).
2. A pregnant mother may go up to 8 months without eating and may lose as much as 43% of her body mass (PB11, COSEWIC 2008 citing Atkinson and Ramsay 1995).
3. Cubs are born at about 0.6 kg with their eyes closed, and leave the den at 10 to 12 kg (PB5, PB6, PB13).
4. Environment Canada says a bear can detect a seal's breathing hole under 90 cm or more of ice and snow, up to a kilometre away (PB43, 2002).
5. Environment Canada says the skin under the white coat is black (PB93, 2002).
6. 73 attacks on people in 144 years (1870 to 2014), 20 deaths, across five countries (PB72, Wilder 2017).
7. Churchill's holding facility has five air-conditioned cells for problem bears (PB68, Manitoba brochure, PDF dated 2010).
8. In 2004 scientists confirmed a polar bear killing and eating a mother in her den, apparently found by scent (PB61, Amstrup 2006).

Runners-up: the 88 degrees north record (PB48); the cub's seal-pup lairs (PB28); bears swimming under seabirds (PB58); bears wait about "every July" on shore (PB49).

## Jokes cannot say

- Any feeling or motive: sad, lonely, proud, bored, "does not like to run" turned into a character trait, a mother's love, a bear "wanting" anything. The sources give actions and counts only.
- A single speed or distance where sources differ: walking pace (5 to 6 km/h in PB44 against 0.5 to 2.1 km/h travel rate in PB45), swim distance (53.7 to 687.1 km in PB35), smell distance (only "about a kilometre" in PB43). No top speed.
- A weight for "a polar bear" (D1) or a litter ("always twins", D2).
- A count of how many cubs live, how many bears are left, or how long they live without the population and years.
- Anything that adds a danger: "they hunt you", "they will eat you", "a bear can smell you from miles".
- Safety advice from the narrator. The only advice row is Manitoba's (PB71). It is said clean, attributed to Manitoba, with the trigger "if you meet a polar bear", and the reaction comes after. Never advice about "play dead" for polar bears next to the grizzly advice in the same line (the Manitoba brochure says "Do not play dead" for polar bears; the master orders handle the grizzly rule separately). No phone number.
- A claim that Churchill is the only place bears meet people (PB66 is Manitoba's boast).
- A forecast: "the ice will be gone by", "bears will disappear", "starving to extinction". Use only dated agency or paper sentences.
- A claim that the video proves the life is a good deal or a bad deal. The verdict is the script's opinion on cited facts.
- "Left-handed", "covers its nose", "20 miles": none has a source (myths table).

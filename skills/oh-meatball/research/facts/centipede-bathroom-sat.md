## Facts

### CB1. They associate with the humid parts of a building, like bathrooms, but turn up in other rooms too
- University of Kentucky Entomology, ENTFACT ef645, "Centipedes and Millipedes in and Around the Home" (Gordon, Larson, DeVries; revised 3/26), https://entomology.mgcafe.uky.edu/ef645 : "Though they are capable of surviving in the comparably drier environment inside a structure, house centipedes do associate with the more humid areas of human-made structures, such as bathrooms, but they can often be found in other rooms inside a home."
- Conditions: say "associate with the more humid areas, like bathrooms" and keep "but they can often turn up in other rooms". Do not say they live only in bathrooms.

### CB2. The only centipede that can survive indoors; it can live its whole life inside
- University of Kentucky ef645 (above): "This is the only species of centipede that can survive inside of structures."
- Ohio State University Extension, ENT-50, Centipedes, https://ohioline.osu.edu/factsheet/ENT-50 : "The house centipede, Scutigera coleoptrata (order Scutigeromorpha), can live its entire life indoors, and it is often found in basements and bathrooms." Also: "Only the house centipede has been known to inhabit buildings year round where they feed primarily on spiders and cockroaches."
- Utah State University Extension, Centipedes (Hodgson, Bingham, Roe, 2008), https://extension.usu.edu/planthealth/research/centipede.pdf : "However, only the house centipede is known to remain inside year round."
- Conditions: "can survive", "can live its entire life". Not "always lives indoors" (Penn State: in summer they can sometimes be found outdoors near buildings).

### CB3. Centipedes have no waxy coating, so they lose water fast in dry air
- Ohio State ENT-50 (above): "Centipedes are unusual among the arthropods (insects, spiders, etc.) because their exoskeletons lack the waxy coating that helps to retain water inside the body. Centipedes require moist environments to survive."
- Sombke, A., Rosenberg, J., Hilken, G., Müller, C. H. G. (2021), "The antennal scape organ of Scutigera coleoptrata (Myriapoda) and a new type of arthropod tip-pore sensilla integrating scolopidial components", Frontiers in Zoology 18:57 (peer-reviewed), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8567564/ : "As their epicuticle is thin and a wax layer is missing, they lose water rapidly at low humidity." (Background; "they" = centipedes; read through WebFetch, which limits quotes to 125 characters, so the checker should read the paragraph in full.)
- Utah State (above): "Centipedes like to be in secluded, damp, and cool places because they easily lose water."
- Conditions: these are about centipedes as a group (the house centipede is one). Say "centipedes", not a number. Do not claim how fast or how many hours.

### CB4. Most centipedes that get into a house die soon from the dry air
- University of Kentucky ef645 (above): "Most millipedes and centipedes will die shortly after entering a structure due to the lack of moisture."
- Ohio State ENT-50 (above): "Most stone and soil centipedes die rather rapidly inside dry buildings."
- Conditions: "most", and about other centipedes, not the house centipede (CB2 is the contrast). The drawn "other centipedes" must look different (stubby), and they tip over, no gore.

### CB5. A small opening at the base of its antennae that may sense humidity (a hypothesis)
- Sombke et al. 2021 (above), abstract: house centipedes (Scutigeromorpha) "possess a peculiar opening at the base of their antenna, termed 'scape organ'"; its sensilla "may be hypothesized to function as hygroreceptors". Background: "Thus, the detection of humid places is vital, but to date no definite hygroreceptor was found and described in detail in centipedes." The paper shows the organ on both the left and right antennae (Figs 1F, 7A) and says: "Very little is known about the cellular organization and function of the scape organ."
- Conditions: "scientists think it may sense humidity; not proven yet". Never "it senses humidity" as a fact. The card says "MAY SENSE HUMIDITY · NOT PROVEN".

### CB6. Nocturnal, very wary, not aggressive; flees when found and hides under anything
- Connecticut Agricultural Experiment Station, "House Centipede (Scutigera coleoptrata)" fact sheet (Katherine Dugas), https://portal.ct.gov/-/media/caes/documents/publications/fact_sheets/entomology/house_centipede_scutigera.pdf : "House centipedes prefer cool, humid, dark environments. In homes they are most likely to frequent basements, cellars, and first-floor bathrooms. They are nocturnal and incredibly wary." And: "Their appearance may be startling due to their long legs and rapid movement, but they are not aggressive and will immediately flee if they are discovered. The centipede's reaction time in these situations is incredibly quick and they will dart away and hide under anything."
- Conditions: the bath mat is the story's "anything". The same page says "Most that live in houses are never seen" (already used: not said) and that they "do not cause harm to ... pets, or humans" (not said: the brief bans the 'harmless' framing).

### CB7. They patrol floors and walls for food; indoors especially in winter
- Missouri Department of Conservation, House Centipede field guide, https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/house-centipede : "House centipedes are usually found inside homes in damp areas like basements, crawl spaces, cellars, and bathrooms, especially in winter, when they move indoors. They patrol floors and walls in search of food, then scurry away when a person approaches."
- Conditions: "especially in winter" (not "only"). "It's November" in the script is the date of the video, said as a joke, not a claim.

### CB8. Menu and barbed legs
- Missouri Department of Conservation (above): "Their diet consists of cockroach nymphs, spiders, silverfish, crickets, bedbugs, flies, moths, and earwigs, many of which are considered household pests." And: "The legs of house centipedes are barbed to help hold prey."
- University of Arkansas Arthropod Museum, House centipede, https://arthropod.uark.edu/house-centipede/ : "In captivity, house centipedes feed readily on cockroach nymphs, flies, moths, bedbugs, crickets, silverfish, earwigs, and other insects and small spiders."
- Conditions: the script names flies, crickets, moths and earwigs (the items not used as punchlines before). Do not say these bugs live in your bathroom.

### CB9. Hunting at night, they can get trapped in a sink or bathtub
- University of Wisconsin Garden Facts XHT1113, "Centipedes" (Phil Pellitteri, UW Insect Diagnostic Lab), https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/files/2014/11/Centipedes.pdf : "Centipedes prefer damp, dark environments and are found outdoors in rotting logs, mulch, potting soil, and leaf litter. They hunt for prey at night and can become trapped if they fall into a sink or bathtub."
- Conditions: "can". The paragraph is about centipedes in general; the sheet's main species is the house centipede. Overlap: Part 5 used the tub as a punchline; here it is a set-up line. The same sheet says the house centipede is "originally from Mexico", which disagrees with Penn State, UKY and Arkansas (Mediterranean): not used.

### CB10. Unlike other centipedes, it can breed indoors, especially where it is moist or humid
- Mississippi State University Extension, Bug's Eye View, House Centipede, No. 29 (2015), https://www.accessibility.extension.msstate.edu/newsletters/bugs-eye-view/2015/house-centipede-no-29 : "Unlike other centipedes, house centipedes are able to breed indoors, especially in areas that are moist or humid."
- Conditions: "can breed", "especially". The same page says "taking over two years to reach maturity" and "rarely very numerous" (the flagship used the single-digits line): neither is said.

### CB11. The bite line (hedged)
- Mississippi State Bug's Eye View No. 29 (above): "Bites to humans are uncommon unless centipedes are handled or accidentally pressed against the skin."
- University of Kentucky ef645 (above): "House centipedes, the most frequently encountered centipede inside the home, pose a minimal bite hazard as their forcipules are so small, that it is difficult for them to successfully pierce human skin."
- Script-safe wording (from `side-hustles/channels/qa/centipede-p4-1117.md`): "Bites are uncommon, and usually only if you handle it or press it against your skin." Never "never bites", "can't bite", "harmless to people".

### CB12. By day it hides in cracks, and centipedes leave no traces
- Utah State University Extension, Centipedes (above): "Centipedes may be difficult to detect because they are usually present in low numbers, exhibit nocturnal behavior, and are highly mobile. Centipedes leave no traces such as feces, eggs or damage to home, furnishings, and plants. During the daytime, centipedes hide in crevices or cracks."
- Conditions: about centipedes in general. Say "centipedes" on the fact lines.

### CB13. Seen often means plenty of what they eat
- Penn State Extension, House Centipedes, https://extension.psu.edu/house-centipedes : "If house centipedes are seen frequently, this indicates that some prey arthropod is in abundance, and may signify a greater problem than the presence of the centipedes." Also: "During the daytime, the centipedes inhabit dark, damp locations in the home and come out at night to forage for prey."
- Conditions: "seen often", not "seen once". Overlap: Part 5 had a sweary version of this; here it answers "what it means for your house".

### CB14. Sticky traps show what is there to eat
- Ohio State ENT-50 (above): "Large numbers of centipedes indoors may indicate that other arthropods used for food are in high numbers. By monitoring with sticky traps, you can observe which and how many arthropods are present in your home."
- Penn State Extension (above): "Determine what other types of arthropods, in your house, are providing a meal for the centipedes by distributing 'sticky insect traps', also called monitors, around the house."
- Conditions: the traps are for finding out which bugs are there. No number of bugs is shown on screen.

### CB15. Dry out damp areas; fans or dehumidifiers
- Utah State (above): "Dry out damp areas in the house (e.g., laundry room, furnace, bathroom, etc.), seal cracks in the foundation and repair any window/door gaps."
- Connecticut Agricultural Experiment Station (above): "To discourage centipedes from appearing out in the open, reduce the humidity levels in open areas by running fans or dehumidifiers."
- University of Arkansas (above): "Indoors, it is an inhabitant of damp places, such as bathrooms, moist closets, and cellars, crawl spaces, and piles of fire wood. Drying and cleaning these areas should help to control infestations of these centipedes."
- Washington State University PestSense, House centipede, https://pestsense.cahnrs.wsu.edu/fact-sheet/house-centipede/ : "House centipedes prefer moist areas. To aid in control, reduce moisture in areas such as basements, bathrooms, etc."
- Conditions: the CT line keeps its purpose ("to keep them from coming out in the open").

### CB16. Bug bombs are ineffective against centipedes
- Ohio State ENT-50 (above): "Total release aerosols (so-called "bug bombs") are also ineffective against centipedes as these products do not leave behind any active residues and they rarely reach the cracks or crevices where centipedes are hiding." And: "Indoor spot treatments are generally not required or recommended."
- Conditions: the can is never shown going off. The description adds "Always read and follow any pesticide label" (OSU: "Always read the label and follow directions and safety precautions.").

### CB17. Can't live with it? Escort it outdoors
- University of Maryland Extension, Centipedes, https://extension.umd.edu/resource/centipedes : "If you see a centipede indoors, and can't live with it, escort it outdoors. Centipedes are beneficial by helping to control insect pests and spiders."
- Conditions: said as Maryland's advice. Penn State says "House centipedes do not survive winters outdoors in Pennsylvania", so the video does not show it going outside in the snow (the gag ends with it back under the mat).

### CB18. Control starts with the bugs it eats and with less moisture
- Mississippi State Bug's Eye View No. 29 (above): "Efforts to control house centipedes should focus on controlling the other household insect pests on which they feed, and on physical exclusion practices that make it more difficult for insects and other arthropod pests to enter the building. Practices that reduce indoor moisture and humidity also aid in control."

## 0. Read this first

- SOLID: every row below is [raw] from one of three agency pages: Alaska Department of Fish and Game (ADF&G, "What to Do About Aggressive Moose"), National Park Service Kenai Fjords "Moose Safety" (page says "Last updated: June 18, 2026"), National Park Service Denali "Wildlife Safety" (page says "Last updated: August 13, 2026") plus the Denali "Moose" page (last updated May 30, 2019).
- The main fact is confirmed today: ADF&G and NPS Kenai Fjords both say more people in Alaska are injured by moose than by bears each year, and both say it is usually a good idea (ADF&G) or "okay" (NPS) to run from a charging moose, unlike with bears.
- NOT GIVEN by any page: a count of injuries, a year, a per-person rate, or a moose speed. The injury line is a ranking ("more than"), not a number. Do not put a number on it.
- CONFLICTS (full list in "Disagreements between sources"): "most" against "many" charges are bluffs; what a knocked-down person may do (ADF&G says hold still, NPS says fighting back backfires; they agree); spray appears on the ADF&G page only; Denali says zigzag, ADF&G says run around something solid.
- Every row is the agency's own advice, with its trigger. These are Alaska and park pages: they are general information, not safety training.

## Three hook facts
1. MO-F1 + MO-F2: in Alaska, more people are injured by moose than by bears each year (two agencies). It is the searched-question twist: people fear the wrong animal. Say "in Alaska" and keep it a ranking, no number.
2. MO-F6 + MO-F7: unlike with bears, running from a moose is usually a good idea, because it won't chase you very far. Two agencies, and it is the opposite of what a viewer was told about bears.
3. MO-F5: a charging moose often kicks forward with its front hooves. It is the visual for Meatball's gag.

## Shortened-claim traps
- "Moose injure more people than bears" drops "in Alaska" and "each year". Both must stay.
- "Run from a moose" drops "usually", "unlike with bears" and "a charging moose". Keep the trigger: a charging moose, not a standing one. For a standing moose the agencies say back away slowly and take cover (ADF&G, NPS Kenai).
- "Curl up in a ball" drops "if it knocks you down". It must keep that trigger on the card and in the line.
- "Hold still" drops "until the moose moves a safe distance away". Moving too soon "may renew its attack".
- "Run behind a tree" must not be shortened into "a tree stops a moose"; ADF&G's reason is only that you can get around it faster.
- "Most charges are bluffs" without "don't wait to find out" turns a safety line into permission to stand still.

## Disagreements between sources
1. Bluff share: ADF&G says "Fortunately most moose charges are bluffs" and, lower on the same page, "Many charges are 'bluff' charges". NPS Kenai says "Most of the time, when a moose charges it is a 'bluff'". Use "many" or "most are bluffs, but don't wait to find out"; never give a percentage, none exists in these pages.
2. Running route: Denali says "Get away as fast as you can, zigzagging and putting obstacles between you and the moose." ADF&G says "Run and get behind something solid" and "you can run around a tree faster than a moose." Same direction (run, with cover). No single technique is the rule.
3. Pepper spray: ADF&G says "As a last resort, pepper spray will often move them, or at least provide some protection if they charge." The NPS Kenai and Denali moose pages do not mention spray. Credit it to ADF&G and keep "last resort"; the short cut does not use it.
4. Moose weight: Denali's safety page says "Moose weigh up to 1,600 lbs"; Denali's moose page says adult males "900-1400 pounds". Use neither as a headline number.
5. Distance: NPS Kenai gives "25 yards (25 m)" and Denali "25 yards (23 m)". 25 yards is about 23 m; Kenai's "25 m" is a rounding. Say 25 yards.
6. The two agencies agree on the knock-down advice. The difference is emphasis: ADF&G describes what the moose may do (stomp and kick with all four feet); NPS Kenai says not to fight back.

# Fact pack: cat-lilies (series 71). Lilies and cats

Prepared for: Meatball Myths Short, "are lilies poisonous to cats". Research date: 2026-11-16.
Prefix: CATL. Searched phrase (from the brief): "are lilies poisonous to cats" (YouTube rank 1, seed "are lilies toxic to"); Google rank 1 "are lilies toxic to cats". I did not re-check the search ranks (no YouTube access allowed); they are the brief's numbers.

How each source was opened:
- [raw] = I fetched the page with curl on 2026-11-16, stripped the tags and copied the quote from that text.
- [abstract] = the journal record's abstract, pulled from the Europe PMC API (the PubMed pages sat behind a cookie wall). The paper itself was not opened.

## 0. Read this first

- SOLID: the main fact. The FDA page (raw) says the whole plant is toxic, including pollen and vase water, and that it can cause fatal kidney failure in less than 3 days. The ASPCA lily pages (raw) say Toxic to Cats, Non-Toxic to Dogs. Pet Poison Helpline (a poison control center, raw) says the same about vase water. A 2010 veterinary review (Fitzgerald, abstract) names both genera, Lilium and Hemerocallis (daylilies), and says the whole plant is toxic.
- The FDA page is dated "Content current as of: 09/16/2021". Not new, but still on the FDA site today.
- The condition that matters: "true lily" (genus Lilium) and daylily (genus Hemerocallis) plants, and cats only. Other plants with "lily" in the name are a different problem (row CATL10).
- The scary numbers are "can" numbers. Two later studies show that with fast treatment most cats do well (rows CATL8, CATL9). The video can be scary and still true: the message is "call the vet right away", not "your cat is doomed".
- Not found: any agency or paper that gives a rate of death for all cats that touch a lily. The two studies below only cover cats that were taken to a vet or reported to a poison center, so they cannot give that rate.
- The toxin is unknown. FDA says "The toxin, which only affects cats, has not been identified." ASPCA lists "Toxic Principles: Unknown."

## 2. Three hook facts

1. CATL2 + CATL1: a few pollen grains licked off its fur, or a sip of vase water, can start fatal kidney failure in a cat in under 3 days (FDA). The whole plant counts, not only the flower.
2. CATL4: after 18 hours or more without treatment the kidney failure is generally permanent (FDA). Call the vet or a pet poison control center right away.
3. CATL5: it is only cats. ASPCA says Toxic to Cats, Non-Toxic to Dogs, so the same bouquet is a minor stomach upset for a dog (FDA) and a possible emergency for the cat.

Backup if a hook needs a number: CATL7 "as little as 2 leaves or part of a single flower have resulted in deaths" (a review of cases, so say "have resulted in deaths", not "will kill").

## 4. Shortened-claim traps

- "Lilies kill cats" loses "true lilies and daylilies" and "can". Keep "can".
- "Lilies are toxic" with no animal loses "to cats" and turns into a claim about dogs or people.
- "Lilies are poisonous to pets" is false for dogs in the kidney sense. Say "cats".
- "The whole lily is deadly, even the water" loses "to cats" and "can cause". Say "toxic to cats".
- "Kidney failure in 3 days" loses "can" and "less than". Say "in under 3 days".
- "18 hours and it is over" loses "generally" and "irreversible kidney failure". Say "after 18 hours the damage is generally permanent".
- "Call poison control" with no trigger: the trigger is "you think your cat ate any part, licked pollen, or drank vase water". Keep the trigger on the card.
- "Peace lilies are safe" would be false (ASPCA lists peace lily as Toxic to Dogs and Cats, mouth irritation). If the gag mentions other lilies, say "a different problem", never "safe".
- Gag card "Meatball read the label": the joke is Meatball carrying the bouquet back out. It must not add a claim (no "it saved Sauce's life"; the bouquet never reached Sauce). Keep the card a reaction, not a fact.

## 5. Disagreements between sources

- "Small amount" (FDA) against "any amount" (Pet Poison Helpline). Same meaning for safety, but only FDA says "small amount". Use FDA's.
- Which plants: FDA lists the most dangerous as the true lilies (Asiatic, Easter, Japanese Show, Oriental, Rubrum, Stargazer, Tiger, Wood) plus daylilies. FDA calls Stargazer "a hybrid"; ASPCA gives Stargazer as Lilium orientalis. Do not give a Latin name on screen.
- Tiger lily: FDA "Lilium tigrinum or lancifolium"; ASPCA "Lilium tigrinum". Do not give a Latin name on screen.
- "Fatal in under 3 days" (FDA) against good outcomes with treatment (Slater 2011: 87% no or brief signs; Bennett 2013: 25 of 25 survived). Not a conflict: different questions (untreated course, treated outcome). The video should say both halves, or at least not deny the second.
- "18 hours" (FDA) against Bennett 2013, where cats were treated up to 48 hours after eating and all survived to discharge. The FDA says "generally"; Bennett's abstract does not give outcome by hours, so I cannot say the two disagree. Do not state 18 hours as a hard cut-off.
- Peace lily: FDA puts it under "Less Harmful" (mouth irritation); ASPCA lists it "Toxic to Dogs, Toxic to Cats" with oral irritation. Same facts, different labels.
- FDA says early signs include "decreased activity level, drooling, vomiting, and loss of appetite"; the ASPCA Easter, Tiger and Stargazer pages list "Vomiting, inappetence, lethargy, kidney failure, and death is possible". ASPCA's lily page says only "Cats: kidney failure." Not a conflict.
- Date: the FDA page says content current as of 09/16/2021. The ASPCA pages show no date.

## 0. Read this first

- SOLID: the main fact. The ASPCA Thanksgiving page opened today and says the sentence word for word (DOGT1, DOGT2). The FDA consumer update opened today and says the trash and carcass line (DOGT4) and the fatty skin line (DOGT6). The AVMA household hazards page opened today and says cooked turkey bones can splinter (DOGT7). Three independent agency or vet-association pages agree: bones are the trigger, and the trash is where the dog gets them.
- The FDA address in the brief did not exist. The guessed "animal-health-literacy" address returned 404. The real page is under Consumer Updates: https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/keep-your-dogs-and-cats-safe-holiday-hazards ("Content current as of: 12/17/2025"). Use that URL.
- NOT re-checked by me: the Google Trends numbers (mean interest 13 in November against 2 in every other month), the YouTube suggestion rank 2 on 2026-11-12, and "0 Shorts found". They came from the research checker in the brief. I have no number of my own for them.
- The brief said the FDA page names "the string" as a hazard in the trash. It does not. On that page the string is in the tinsel and ribbons paragraph ("leftover tinsel, string, and ribbons"), not in the trash paragraph. The trash paragraph names the turkey carcass and steak bones. Do not put string in this video.
- The brief said "fatty skin and gravy" from the FDA. The FDA page names fat trimmings and roasted turkey skin. It does not name gravy. Gravy appears on the ASPCA page, with a different reason (sodium in bouillon cubes). Keep them apart (see traps).
- Not measured anywhere in the sources: how many dogs get hurt by turkey bones. None of the three pages gives a count or a percentage. So this pack has no statistics, only agency instructions.

### Three hook facts

1. DOGT1 + DOGT2: the ASPCA's own line, "boneless and fully cooked", with the reason, "poultry bones can be a major choking hazard". The condition is boneless, fully cooked and a small bite. No number.
2. DOGT4: the FDA calls dogs "notorious" for helping themselves to the turkey carcass out of the trash. The agency's own action: wrap the scraps and bones and put them in a bin the dog cannot get into.
3. DOGT7: the AVMA says cooked turkey bones can splinter. This breaks the belief that cooked bones are safer. No number.

None of the hooks has a number. The sources have no statistic on turkey and dogs. Do not invent one (no "x dogs a year").

## 3. Shortened-claim traps

- "Dogs can eat turkey" (drops: small bite, boneless, fully cooked, "if you decide to", ask your vet). The most likely cut, and false as a bare line.
- "Boneless and fully cooked" with no "if you share" reads as an order to share. Keep "if you share turkey".
- "Cooked turkey is safe" (drops "boneless"). Cooked bones splinter (DOGT7).
- "The FDA says turkey skin is bad" is close, but the true line is fatty scraps such as roasted skin and fat trimmings "can upset the stomach and cause pancreatitis". Keep "fatty" and the "don't give".
- "Don't let your dog near the trash" cut from "securely wrap up the scraps and bones and put them in a bin your pet can't get into". Keep the bones and the bin.
- "Bones get stuck" with no "can" turns a possibility into a rule.
- "Gravy upsets a dog's stomach" is a mix of two sources. The only gravy line is about bouillon cubes and sodium.
- "The ASPCA says skip the turkey" is wrong too. It says stick to the normal diet and offers a boneless small bite for those who decide to share.
- "Ask your vet" is the vet or the agency's action only where the sources send you there ("When in doubt, call your veterinarian"; ASPCA: contact your veterinarian or ASPCA Poison Control "if you suspect your pet may have ingested a toxic substance"). A bone is not a "toxic substance" on the ASPCA's page, so do not attach the Poison Control phone number to the bone line.

## 4. Disagreements between sources

- ASPCA Thanksgiving page, two lines that pull apart. "Just a Taste" says to stick to the normal diet and, if you share, a small boneless fully cooked bite. "A Feast Fit for a King" says to "give your cat and dog a small feast of their own. try mixing some pieces of turkey, sweet potatoes and green beans into their usual dinner. You can even top it off with a drizzle of gravy." The second is a separate tip and does not repeat the bones and cooked line. It also tells you to top with gravy, while the same page flags bouillon in gravy. Use only the "Just a Taste" lines; do not say the ASPCA says to drizzle gravy.
- Bones. ASPCA: "boneless", poultry bones a choking hazard. FDA: carcass and steak bones can stick; sharp pieces can injure. AVMA: cooked turkey bones can splinter. These agree. They name different harms (choking, getting stuck, splintering). Do not merge them into one harm.
- Who is covered. The ASPCA turkey line says "your pet" (the page speaks of "your cat and dog"); the FDA trash and carcass line says "your dog"; the FDA skin line says "your pet". The AVMA line says "your pet". The video is about the dog, so say "dog" only where the FDA says dog (carcass, bones in the trash). For the fatty skin line and the ASPCA lines say "pet" or attribute them as written.
- No page gives a number for how often any of this happens. There is no conflict because none of them measures it.

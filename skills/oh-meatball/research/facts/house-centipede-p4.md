# Fact pack: House centipede Part 4 (TikTok, 11/17)

For `scripts/drawn-house/tt-house-centipede-p4.json`. Written 2026-11-17. All pages were opened that day. NOT yet checked by a second model.

**Already used in Parts 1-5, so not main facts here:** 15 pairs of legs; feelers that look like legs ("which end is the face"); "fast runner" with no number; the venom fangs and "almost never stings you"; the prey list; out at night, most never seen, hides in basements, cellars and bathrooms; hatches with 4 pairs of legs and adds more at each molt; 3 years to grow up, up to 7 years of life; the female's back legs; up to 150 young; body 1 to 1.5 in, 3 to 4 in with legs; Mediterranean, PA 1849; drops a leg and regrows it; floor drains, cardboard, crawl spaces; catches several bugs at once and eats one while holding the others (Part 5); stuck in the tub; lots of them may mean lots of other bugs; dry out the damp; harmless to pets and the house; a bite like a bee sting.

**About the main source.** Acosta, C. A. (2003), "The House Centipede (Scutigera coleoptrata; Chilopoda): Controversy and Contradiction", *Journal of the Kentucky Academy of Science* 64(1):1-5. The author is in the Department of Biological Sciences, Northern Kentucky University, and the PDF is on NKU's own site (the file is named 2000, but the scan says Volume 64, Spring 2003). It is a review, so most of its facts cite older studies (named below). The PDF is a scan: in the quotes below, OCR slips are fixed silently, and a word missing from the scan is in [brackets].

## Facts

### P4-F1. Clocked at about 16 inches a second; listed in the 1973 Guinness Book
- Northern Kentucky University (Acosta 2003), https://websites.nku.edu/~acostac/AcostaJKAS2000.PDF : "House centipedes are the greyhounds of the Myriapoda. They have been clocked at 420 mm./sec with a 33 mm stride, which was impressive enough to be listed as among the fastest arthropods in the 1973 Guinness Book of World Records."
- Mississippi State University Extension, Bug's Eye View, House Centipede (No. 29, 2015), https://www.accessibility.extension.msstate.edu/newsletters/bugs-eye-view/2015/house-centipede-no-29 : "They are also able to run much faster than most other ground-dwelling arthropods, reaching speeds approaching one MPH."
- Conditions: 420 mm/s is about 16.5 inches a second; say "about sixteen inches a second". It was listed "among the fastest arthropods", in the 1973 edition: do not say it holds a record or is the fastest. "Approaching one MPH" means close to, not over.
- New: Parts 2 and 3 said only "fast" and "one of the fastest things in your house". The number and the Guinness listing were not used.

### P4-F2. Darts, stops dead, freezes; runs up walls to hunt
- University of Arkansas Arthropod Museum, House centipede, https://arthropod.uark.edu/house-centipede/ : "The house centipede is often seen darting across floors at high speed, occasionally stopping suddenly and remaining motionless for some time before racing off once more."
- University of Kentucky Entomology, fact sheet ef645, "Centipedes and Millipedes in and Around the Home" (J. Gordon, J. L. Larson, Z. DeVries), https://entomology.mgcafe.uky.edu/ef645 : "To aid in hunting, house centipedes can run very quickly across floors and up walls."
- Conditions: "occasionally" and "for some time", so say "can freeze for a while". No source here says ceilings; the ceiling in the script is Meatball looking there, not a claim.

### P4-F3. Catches prey by half pouncing, half lassoing
- University of Arkansas Arthropod Museum, https://arthropod.uark.edu/house-centipede/ : "They capture prey by half pouncing and half lassoing them."
- Acosta 2003 (NKU, above): "The long legs of S. coleoptrata function primarily for chasing and catching mobile prey."
- Conditions: the same Arkansas paragraph has the "several prey at once, eats one while holding the others" fact that Part 5 used. Use only the pounce and lasso.

### P4-F4. Big faceted (compound) eyes, rare among centipedes
- University of Arkansas Arthropod Museum, https://arthropod.uark.edu/house-centipede/ : "Unlike most other centipedes, house centipedes and their close relatives have well-developed, faceted eyes."
- Acosta 2003 (NKU, above): "House centipedes have large heads, prominent compound eyes, long annulated antennae, and very long legs." Also: "Behind the antennae is a pair of modified compound eyes, in contrast to other chilopods which have simple ocelli."
- Conditions: "unlike most other centipedes" (its close relatives have them too). The eyes are "modified" compound eyes; Acosta compares the lens to the eyes of insects and crustaceans, but the script does not use that comparison. The number of units per eye is not used: the scan cannot be read cleanly there.

### P4-F5. It grooms on a strict schedule, legs through its mouthparts, and still cleans legs it has lost
- Acosta 2003 (NKU, above), citing Le Moli and Parmigiani (1976) and Verhoeff (1938b): "House centipedes meticulously groom themselves on a strict schedule (Le Moli and Parmigiani 1976). The antennae and legs are gripped by the forcipules and passed through the cleaning setae on the first maxillae (Verhoeff 1938b)."
- Same page: "Grooming activity starts with an antenna, followed by legs 1 through 15 of the same side. Appendages on the other side are then cleaned in the same order. If an individual is interrupted for any reason during grooming, cleaning commences where the individual left off prior to being disturbed."
- Same page: "Grooming behavior appears to be genetically hard-wired because house centipedes will still attempt to clean legs that have been amputated."
- Conditions: the first maxillae are mouthparts, so "through its mouthparts" is right; "through its mouth" is not. "Attempt to clean" means it tries. "Like floss" is a simile for the picture, not a claim. The studies are old (1938, 1976) and reach us through a review.

### P4-F6. It breathes through holes down the middle of its back
- Acosta 2003 (NKU, above): "The seven tergites each bear a median dorsal spiracle for gas exchange, unlike the lateral paired spiracles of other myriapods."
- Conditions: tergites are the back plates (Acosta: "the terga are fused into seven plates"), dorsal means on the back, median means down the middle, a spiracle is a breathing opening. "Other myriapods" (centipedes and millipedes) have them in pairs along the sides. "Seven" is sourced but not said in the script.

### P4-F7. Females lay their eggs one at a time, in soil, in spring and early summer
- Acosta 2003 (NKU, above), citing Lewis (1981): "Females [lay] relatively few eggs (average four eggs per day; Lewis 1981), singly in soil. Laying and hatching occurs from late spring through early summer."
- University of Arkansas Arthropod Museum, https://arthropod.uark.edu/house-centipede/ : "Eggs are laid in spring and early summer." The same page: "In laboratory rearings, 24 females produced an average of 63 eggs each and a maximum of 151 eggs."
- Conditions: the verb is lost in the scan; "singly in soil" is printed clearly. Part 4-v6 already used "up to 150 young" (Penn State), so the 151 maximum is not new. The average of 63 (lab rearings) is new but not used in the script.

### P4-F8. A male that has just molted is especially likely to be eaten by a female
- Acosta 2003 (NKU, above): "They are also known to kill other centipedes including Bothropolys and Lithobius, as well as other scutigerids. Newly molted male house centipedes are especially susceptible to predation by females."
- Conditions: "especially susceptible", not "always eaten". Nothing says it happens during mating, so no joke may tie it to a date with the centipede; the script's joke is Meatball cancelling his own date.

## Spares (sourced, not in the script)
- Vision: Acosta 2003: "Le Moli (1970) suggested that S. coleoptrata was able to visually distinguish between certain mutant types of the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster." Say "one old study suggested" and "certain" fruit flies. Cut for length.
- Few at a time: Mississippi State Extension (above): "Because of this long life cycle, and the fact that they are predators, house centipedes are rarely very numerous. Most infestations can be counted in single digits, rather than hundreds." Cut for length; a good Part 5 line.
- Rear legs as feelers: Acosta 2003: the last pair of legs "does not appear to function for locomotion but instead may serve as 'rear antennae.'" Close to Part 1's "feelers look like legs", so held back.

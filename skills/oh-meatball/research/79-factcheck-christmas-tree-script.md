Independent second-reviewer check of `scripts/longform-christmas-tree-check.outline.json` (96 narration lines, with their cards, checks and the `vis` notes that put a claim on screen) against fact pack `research/77-christmas-tree-bugs-factpack.md` and against the live sources. ## 0. Verdict

- **OK: 78 lines. SOFTEN: 18 lines (1 of them check-card only). CUT: 0.** Plus 2 new lines (and 1 optional) to add to the lanternfly segment.
- Nothing in the pack's "Do NOT say" list is said head-on: 25,000 is framed as "as many as ... in some trees", 1 in 100,000 keeps "perhaps", egg counts are per case, no hatch time in days, no garage hold time, no "spray is fine", no "eats your furniture", no fake-tree claim.

The three most serious problems:

1. **The count in the cold open is wrong (line 6, and title 3).** The body delivers six "ignore" stowaways (3 to 8), one "vacuum, never squash" (the aphid), one "clip" (mantis) and one "outside" (lanternfly). "Seven you can ignore" counts the aphid, whose own segment says "Here is the one rule. Do not squash them." Line 68's picture also shows "all six silhouettes".
2. **The lanternfly segment overstated the risk and gave no action (lines 73, 76, 77).** Penn State's current page (updated 19 January 2026) says "Although unlikely, spotted lanternfly eggs can occur on Christmas trees". Penn State's experts call the risk "low" because Christmas trees "are not a preferred host". "Penn State says the egg masses can ride on trees from quarantine zones" drops the "unlikely". The segment also never says what to do with an egg mass. Every current source says to scrape it into alcohol or hand sanitizer, or smash it, and to report it if you are outside the known range. The guidance is still current in 2026, so the segment stays, softened and with those two lines added.
3. **Three lines misread their source.** Line 11 ("Mostly pine"): the Bergen article says there are "particularly much" bugs in pine, not that the trees were mostly pine. Naturalis says the trees were "spruces and pines". Line 50 ("feed only on firs"): NC State's Cinara page does say this, but the same page says Cinara feed "on many conifers including Fraser fir and white pines", and Penn State says they are found "on most species of conifer". Line 83 ("Shaking is the part everyone agrees on"): the Norwegian biologist's shake was a way to look at the bugs, not advice to remove them, and "everyone" is broader than the sources.

Also important: the TREE CHECK numbering. Item 3 appears in the body as "SHAKE IT, AND CHECK THE TRUNK" (line 77), but the final list makes 3 "shake" and 4 "nest and trunk". Item 5's card says "VACUUM. NEVER SQUASH." while the final step 5 adds "never spray". Fixes are in the table (lines 77, 80, 83).

## 2. Lanternfly segment

**Is the Christmas-tree concern still current in 2026?** Yes, but as a low-risk, local issue. It is not specific to 2019.

- Penn State Extension, "Insects on Real Christmas Trees" (https://extension.psu.edu/insects-on-real-christmas-trees), shows "Updated: January 19, 2026" and still has the lanternfly section: "Although unlikely, spotted lanternfly eggs can occur on Christmas trees." Its range figures are still "as of December 2019", with "be sure to double-check the latest range map as this publication ages". **Do not quote those county counts.** Same page: "An egg mass that is brought indoors may hatch in a few weeks" and the nymphs "will quickly die due to starvation and low indoor humidity"; "residents in and around the quarantine zone are encouraged to dispose of old Christmas trees via community tree recycling programs (which generally chip old trees) or burning"; "If a Christmas tree is thrown outside before a spotted lanternfly egg mass hatches indoors, it could hatch in the spring and spread the population"; "it is imperative that visual inspection of the Christmas tree be carried out before transporting your tree"; "If you live outside the quarantine zone and spotted lanternflies hatch indoors from a Christmas tree, please report the incident to ... your state agricultural agency". General line on the same page: "Insect egg masses, such as those laid by mantises, gypsy moths, and spotted lanternfly, can be scraped off the tree trunk or branches."
- Penn State News, 18 Nov 2019, "Extension experts: Risk of spotted lanternfly on Christmas trees is minimal" (https://www.psu.edu/news/impact/story/extension-experts-risk-spotted-lanternfly-christmas-trees-minimal): "Christmas trees are not a preferred host for spotted lanternflies, so the probability of finding a spotted lanternfly or an egg mass on Christmas trees is low"; "Egg masses, which resemble gray mud splatters, can be scraped easily from tree bark"; destroy them "by placing them in a container filled with rubbing alcohol or hand sanitizer ... they also can be smashed or burned".
- Penn State Extension, "Spotted Lanternfly Management Guide" (https://extension.psu.edu/spotted-lanternfly-management-guide, updated 10 June 2024): egg masses "are protected with a mud-like covering", "covered with a gray-brown substance, flat"; "SLF eggs can be destroyed by scraping them ... into an alcohol solution (e.g., rubbing alcohol or hand sanitizer) where they should be left permanently ... Eggs can also be destroyed by thoroughly smashing them"; "Slow the spread of SLF by checking your vehicle and any outdoor equipment (furniture, mowers, firewood, etc.) when going in and out of the quarantine zone"; "If you find SLF in new areas where they have not been found before, be sure to report it to your state's agriculture authorities or by calling 1-888-4BAD-FLY."
- Virginia Cooperative Extension ENTO-402NP (2021), "Best Management Practices for Spotted Lanternfly on Christmas Tree Farms" (https://turf.spes.vt.edu/content/dam/pubs_ext_vt_edu/ENTO/ento-402/ENTO-402.pdf): "The Spotted Lanternfly (SLF) is not a pest of conifers, but the movement of Christmas trees may accidentally spread SLF"; "There is a slight risk that fresh Christmas trees may harbor SLF egg masses"; "Shaking cut trees should dislodge any adult SLF ... but it will not dislodge any egg masses" (so the trunk check is a separate step from the shake); "the homeowner should contact the local Cooperative Extension office to report the find."
- USDA APHIS, Spotted Lanternfly (https://www.aphis.usda.gov/plant-pests-diseases/slf, "Last Modified: August 26, 2025"). curl got 403. Read through the fetch tool's summarizer only: lays eggs "on any hard surface including grills, vehicles, trailers, firewood, outdoor furniture"; newly laid egg masses "resemble wet, gray putty" and then dull and turn brown, about 1 inch long; scrape "into a plastic zippered bag filled with hand sanitizer or rubbing alcohol"; "Residents in spotted lanternfly quarantine areas should check outdoor items before traveling to new areas"; "Immediately report your findings". The page does not mention Christmas trees.
- New Jersey Dept. of Agriculture homeowner page (https://www.nj.gov/agriculture/divisions/pi/prog/pests-diseases/spotted-lanternfly/homeowner-resources/): "Inspecting your vehicles, trailers, or any outdoor items before you move around or out of a quarantine zone is important"; "Scrape egg masses into a plastic bag and place in trash"; "All New Jersey counties are now quarantined".

**Does what to do depend on being inside or outside a quarantine zone?** Destroying an egg mass (scrape into alcohol or sanitizer, or smash) applies everywhere. Reporting is asked of people outside the known range (Penn State, APHIS). Inside a zone, the extra step is not to move items out unchecked and to chip or burn the tree after the holidays, not dump it outdoors (Penn State).

## 5. Titles and the nine-count

**Nine-count.** The body delivers 9: (1) mantis = clip; (2) aphid = vacuum, never squash; (3) spiders, (4) bark beetles, (5) adelgids, (6) scale crawlers, (7) mites, (8) bark lice = ignore (six); (9) lanternfly = check the trunk and matters outside. "Ignore" is fair for all six: Penn State's rule is to leave them until the tree goes out, and to wipe up scale crawlers or vacuum spider webs off the tree. The bird nest in 7 is an action, but it is about the nest, not the mites. So the cold open must read six, not seven (line 6 fix).

1. **"Never Bring a Christmas Tree Inside Before You Check for This"**: OK, with a note. The check is exactly what the sources advise. Penn State: "The best control is to look for and remove any egg masses before a tree is brought indoors". Illinois: "Scout for mantis eggs before bringing the tree indoors". The title makes no claim about how often egg cases turn up, and the script itself says they are rare (line 17) and that the panic level is one. No change required.
2. **"Check Your Christmas Tree for This Before It Comes in Your House"**: OK. The safest of the three if "Never" feels too strong.
3. **"9 Things Hiding in Your Christmas Tree (Clip One, Ignore Seven)"**: SOFTEN. "Ignore Seven" is wrong (six), and "Hiding in Your Christmas Tree" implies they are in the viewer's tree, while Penn State says most trees are free of them. Use: **"9 Things That Can Ride In on Your Christmas Tree (Clip One, Ignore Six)"**.
